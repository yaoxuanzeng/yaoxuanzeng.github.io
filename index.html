I am an MIT School of Science Postdoctoral Fellow in the Department of Earth, Atmospheric and Planetary Sciences, working with Wanying Kang.

I study the fluid dynamics of planetary atmospheres and oceans, focusing on how rotation, stratification, and buoyancy shape circulation and transport. My research spans ocean circulation and convection on icy moons, jet formation on giant planets, and ocean dynamics on tidally locked exoplanets. At MIT, I am particularly interested in the dynamics and tracer transport of icy moon oceans.

I received my Ph.D. in Geophysical Sciences from the University of Chicago in 2026, working with Malte F. Jansen, and my B.S. in Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences from Peking University in 2020, working with Jun Yang. I was also a GFD Fellow at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in summer 2023.

Research

I use theory and numerical simulations to explore how rotation, buoyancy, and turbulence shape planetary oceans and atmospheres.

Icy moon oceans

Saturn’s moon Enceladus harbors a liquid-water ocean beneath its icy shell. Heat from the rocky interior and freezing and melting at the ice–ocean interface help drive circulation in this hidden ocean, connecting the seafloor to the ice above.

I investigate how salinity, rotation, and convection shape ocean circulation and heat transport on icy moons. My work examines interactions between the ocean and ice shell, how to represent slantwise convection in global models, and how convection and baroclinic eddies interact. I am also interested in how these flows transport tracers.

Related papers Salinity · Ice-ocean interaction · Instability analysis · Convection scaling · Convection parameterization

Giant planet atmospheres

The atmospheres of giant planets organize into powerful east–west jets. Jupiter and Saturn have eastward equatorial jets, while Uranus and Neptune have westward ones. Their visible cloud patterns offer a window into fluid motions that extend beneath the cloud tops.

I study how the depth of atmospheric flows affects the formation and organization of planetary jets. Using idealized models, I investigate what controls the direction of the equatorial jet, the number and spacing of jets, and the distribution of energy between jets and turbulent eddies.

Related paper Jet depth & direction

Tidally locked planets

TRAPPIST-1 hosts seven roughly Earth-sized planets in close orbits around a cool dwarf star. Such close-in planets are expected to be tidally locked, with permanent day and night sides. If surface oceans are present, they experience a pattern of heating very different from Earth’s.

I investigate ocean circulation on idealized tidally locked terrestrial planets, focusing on superrotation—an eastward equatorial current relative to the surface. My research explores how winds, ocean waves, and planetary rotation govern its development and maintenance, as well as how ocean and sea-ice dynamics affect heat redistribution and climate.

Related papers Oceanic superrotation · Sea-ice drift
